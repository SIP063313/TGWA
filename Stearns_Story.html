Eric Gordon Stearns

b. 03 Feb 1895 d. 06 Aug 1915

Eric Gordon Stearns was born at Hornsey on 25 March 1895, the younger son of Thomas Robert and Alice Stearns. He spent his early years in Stoke Newington before the family settled at Lansdowne, Woodridings, Hatch End. His education began at St. John's College before he attended the Lower School at Harrow, where he became well known both academically and socially.

As The Lyonian reported on the year he left the school “Of all the boys who left their old School at Christmas, we want to say a few words of kindly farewell." Eric Stearns, who was a Lyonian almost from babyhood—at least, he was wonderfully small when first I saw him—has left as Monitor, member of Football 1st XI., and Six-footer. Besides that, we are in hopes that he has added the London Matriculation to his list of honours. E. S. was a really capital actor, and, alongside his bosom friend, Leo Parsons, made no small stir when he first appeared in "Twelfth Night." Into the bargain, he was always a rare good Lyonian, with an unmistakable touch of the public school boy about him, and that is no bad thing. We believe that he is about to enter into the "Sun" Insurance Office; best wishes to him.

After leaving school he entered civilian employment as a clerk with the Sun Insurance Office and, in 1912, also joined the Artists' Rifles. With the outbreak of the First World War, Stearns volunteered for overseas service. On 6 April 1915 he was commissioned as a Second Lieutenant in the 4th Battalion, Royal Fusiliers, and served on the Western Front. During operations in the Ypres Salient he was placed in command of a party digging a new frontline trench. While directing the work he was wounded by a bullet above the hip and was taken to the medical facilities at Abeele, where he died of his wounds on 6 August 1915, aged twenty.

He was buried in Lijssenthoek Military Cemetery, Belgium.

The surviving contemporary accounts provide a record of how Eric Stearns was regarded by those who served with him. His commanding officer, Lieutenant-Colonel W. F. Sweny, wrote that he had "seen enough of your son to realise what a useful and cool officer he was." Sergeant F. H. Smith wrote that Stearns was "almost worshipped by the men under his command," adding that "they would have followed him anywhere," that "he had not an atom of fear in him," and that he always had "a smile and a cheery word for everybody." Private W. H. Leighton, who was with him after he was wounded, wrote that "we did everything we possibly could but all in vain," adding that Stearns "bore it extremely well" and was "one whom the men had the greatest confidence in and would have followed anywhere."

Newspaper tributes echoed these sentiments, recording that he was "always popular with his men," "highly thought of by his senior officers," and that "his men would have done anything for him." These contemporary accounts form the documented record of Eric Gordon Stearns' military service and the esteem in which he was held by those who served alongside him.
