Echoes of Sacrifice - His Story

Driver J.P. Williams (Service Number 624194) of the Honourable Artillery Company did not die in World War I; he survived the war. Service Number 624194 belongs to a soldier who was wounded in combat but survived his injuries. Because he survived, his name does not appear on the Commonwealth War Graves Commission (CWGC) rolls or any WWI casualty lists of the deceased. Instead, he was honorably discharged from the British Army due to medical unfitness caused by his wartime wounds.

The Silver War Badge: Upon his discharge, he was awarded the Silver War Badge (SWB). This was a brass medal issued to British and Commonwealth military personnel who were safely discharged due to wounds or sickness, meant to be worn on civilian clothes to show they had served honorably.

The 126th Brigade, Royal Field Artillery operated primarily on the Western Front during World War I. The brigade was originally raised as a "New Army" unit and was assigned to provide tactical artillery support for the 37th Division, arriving in France in July 1915.

In January 1917, as part of a British Army-wide reorganization, the original 126th Brigade was broken up and its batteries distributed. In early 1917, the brigade was completely reformed as an independent Army Field Artillery Brigade.

During this period, it absorbed prestigious Territorial units, specifically the 2/A and 2/B Batteries of the Honourable Artillery Company (HAC). It operated heavily in the Ypres Salient during the Battle of Passchendaele, and later participated in holding the line against the German Spring Offensive of March 1918.
