William “Willie” Oliver

b. 1884 d. 18 Nov 1916

William “Willie” Oliver worked as a bank clerk and was known for his artistic ability at John Lyon School, before enlisting in the London Irish Rifles during the First World War. He was killed in an accident near Poperinghe in October 1916, aged 32, when he was struck by a light railway engine while detraining.

Civilian Life - Willie Oliver was born in Launceston, Cornwall, in 1884, the eldest son of George Massey and Annie Laura Simcoe Oliver. While he was still young, the family settled in Harrow, where his father worked as a clerk. Willie attended The John Lyon School before beginning a career as a clerk with the National Bank at its Camden Town branch.

Away from his professional work, he was remembered for an exceptional artistic ability. Former school records described him as "an inveterate sketcher" and noted that he was ambidextrous, a rare skill that enabled him to draw with either hand. Although no collection of his work is known to survive, this gift was one of the qualities for which he was remembered by those who knew him.

Military Life - When the First World War intervened, Willie exchanged the drawing pencil for a rifle. Enlisting as Rifleman 5077 in the 1st/18th Battalion, London Regiment (London Irish Rifles), he joined a battalion that, in October 1916, moved from the Somme to the Ypres Salient in Belgium. His time on the Western Front was remarkably brief. Contemporary reports record that he had been in France for only four days when, after his battalion had been relieved from the line and transported by rail in darkness and heavy rain, tragedy struck.

While the men were detraining near Poperinghe, a light railway engine emerged unexpectedly. Despite warning shouts, Willie failed to clear the track in time and was struck and killed. His death occurred not in an attack or under enemy fire, but in an accident while on active service, bringing his military career to an abrupt end almost as soon as it had begun. He was buried in Vlamertinghe Military Cemetery near Ypres.

His father received a kind letter from the Rev. Father Pike saying: “I have buried your brave son in a beautifully kept Cemetery and gave him a Catholic burial and blessed his grave with many kind expressions of sympathy and deep regrets from his C.O. His commanding officer later wrote to his parents that he had always carried out every instruction faithfully and that he would be greatly missed by the battalion.
