Son of Thos. R. Stearns, of Lansdowne, Woodridings, Hatch End, Middlesex
Lieutenant Eric Gordon Stearns died on August 6, 1915, at the young age of 20. He served with the 4th Battalion of the Royal Fusiliers (City of London Regiment).  Lieutenant Stearns died at Abeele, Belgium, a village located west of Ypres right along the French-Belgian border.He is buried at the Commonwealth War Graves Commission (CWGC) Loker Churchyard in West-Vlaanderen, Belgium (Grave Reference: II. C. 20.).  He died of wounds received in action in Belgium.     While serving in the Ypres sector, Lieutenant Stearns was in charge of an engineering and infantry party tasked with digging a new frontline trench under the cover of darkness.  Frontline digging parties were incredibly hazardous roles, exposed to sudden enemy flare lights, targeted machine-gun sweeps, and pre-ranged sniper fire. During the operation, his party was caught in an exchange, and Stearns was severely wounded. He was evacuated behind the immediate lines to a medical post or field ambulance in the Abeele sector, where he succumbed to his injuries.  Historical logs and letters from his commanding officer, Lieut.-Col. W. F. Sweny, and his men note that Stearns was highly regarded as an exceptionally cool, fearless leader who was "almost worshipped by the men under his command" for always keeping a cheery word for everyone despite the intense conditions

Eric Gordon Stearns: Civilian and Military Life

Eric Gordon Stearns was born at Hornsey on 25 March 1895, the younger son of Thomas Robert and Alice Stearns. He spent his early years in Stoke Newington before the family settled at Lansdowne, Woodridings, Hatch End. His education began at St. John's College before he attended the Lower School at Harrow, where he became well known both academically and socially. As The Lyonian reported on the year he left the school “Of all the boys who left their old School at Christmas, we want to say a few words of kindly farewell. Eric Stearns, who was a Lyonian almost from babyhood—at least, he was wonderfully small when first I saw him—has left as Monitor, member of Football 1st XI., and Six-footer. Besides that, we are in hopes that he has added the London Matriculation to his list of honours. E. S. was a really capital actor, and, alongside his bosom friend, Leo Parsons, made no small stir when he first appeared in "Twelfth Night." Into the bargain, he was always a rare good Lyonian, with an unmistakable touch of the public school boy about him, and that is no bad thing. We believe that he is about to enter into the "Sun" Insurance Office; best wishes to him.

After leaving school he entered civilian employment as a clerk with the Sun Insurance Office and, in 1912, also joined the Artists' Rifles.
With the outbreak of the First World War, Stearns volunteered for overseas service. On 6 April 1915 he was commissioned as a Second Lieutenant in the 4th Battalion, Royal Fusiliers, and served on the Western Front. During operations in the Ypres Salient he was placed in command of a party digging a new frontline trench. While directing the work he was wounded by a bullet above the hip and was taken to the medical facilities at Abeele, where he died of his wounds on 6 August 1915, aged twenty. He was buried in Lijssenthoek Military Cemetery, Belgium.
The surviving contemporary accounts provide a record of how Eric Stearns was regarded by those who served with him. His commanding officer, Lieutenant-Colonel W. F. Sweny, wrote that he had "seen enough of your son to realise what a useful and cool officer he was." Sergeant F. H. Smith wrote that Stearns was "almost worshipped by the men under his command," adding that "they would have followed him anywhere," that "he had not an atom of fear in him," and that he always had "a smile and a cheery word for everybody." Private W. H. Leighton, who was with him after he was wounded, wrote that "we did everything we possibly could but all in vain," adding that Stearns "bore it extremely well" and was "one whom the men had the greatest confidence in and would have followed anywhere." Newspaper tributes echoed these sentiments, recording that he was "always popular with his men," "highly thought of by his senior officers," and that "his men would have done anything for him." These contemporary accounts form the documented record of Eric Gordon Stearns' military service and the esteem in which he was held by those who served alongside him.

The Observer Friday October 22 1915

 

 

LEUT. ERIC STEARNS.

TRIBUTES FROM ALL RANKS.

The sad news of Lieut. Eric Stearns' death was received on Friday last. The young officer, who belonged to the 11th Battalion, had been at the Front for about a month.

A brother officer of the Battalion writes:— "He was a good officer, and was always popular with his men, and he is a loss to us. He died doing his duty, and his memory will live long with us."

Another letter from a comrade says:— "Lieut. Stearns was killed on Friday night. He was in command of a party working at the Front, when a shell exploded close to him, killing him instantly. He was a brave fellow, and was popular with all ranks."

The Company Commander writes:— "I cannot tell you how much I feel his loss; it is a personal sorrow to me. Since he joined the Battalion, he was always by my side. The Company was managing a difficult piece of work, and he was in the firing line at south of night. There was a great heavy fire, during which much of the work was destroyed, and Eric was killed on the right side."

The Chaplain of the forces, writing to the parents, says:— "I feel I must write to you to express my deep sympathy with you in your great sorrow. Your son was killed on the night of the 15th October. He was in command of a party working at the Front, and was struck by a piece of shell. His death was instantaneous, so that he did not suffer. I buried him on Saturday night in the cemetery near here, and a cross is being erected to his memory. It may comfort you to know that he was a good soldier, and was highly thought of by his senior officers. His men would have done anything for him, and speak of his courage and coolness in the hour of danger. They all feel his loss deeply."

STEARNS, ERIC GORDON, 2nd Lieut., 4th Battn. Royal Fusiliers, yr. s. of Thomas Robert Stearns, of Lansdowne, Woodridings, Hatch End, co. Middlesex, Insurance Official, by his wife, Alice, dau. of James Grant, of Ipswich and Belize; b. Hornsey, 25 March, 1895; educ. St. John's College, and Lower School, Harrow; was a Clerk in the Sun Insurance Office; joined the Artists' Rifles (28th London Regt.) in 1912; volunteered for foreign service on the outbreak of war; was given a commission as 2nd Lieut. 6 April, 1915, and posted to the Royal Fusiliers; went to France in Oct. 1914, and died at Abeele of wounds received in action in Belgium, while in charge of a party digging a new trench; unm. His commanding officer, Lieut.-Col. W. F. Sweny, wrote: "I have seen enough of your son to realise what a useful and cool officer he was"; Sergt. F. H. Smith: "Mr. Stearns was almost worshipped by the men under his command; they would have followed him anywhere, for they knew he was a man. He had not an atom of fear in him, and he had always a smile and a cheery word for everybody. I do not think anybody could be more liked and respected than he was and Private W. H. Leighton: "The bullet caught him just above the hip and passed upwards. We did everything we possibly could but all in vain, worse luck. He bore it extremely well, as he always did everything, being a very brave officer and one whom the men had the greatest confidence in and would have followed him anywhere."

HARROW OBSERVER - 03 JANUARY 1919

REQUIEM AT ST. ANSELM’S.

COMMEMORATION SERVICE.

In accordance with the wish of the Archbishops of Canterbury and York, the last Sunday in the year was set apart for commemoration of the brave souls who, during the great European War, have given their lives for King and country. The services at St. Anselm's, Hatch End, began with a celebration of the Eucharist at 8. This service partook of a commemorative character, with special collect, Epistle and Gospel. Communion was given to a number of communicants who had responded to the invitation to plead the cause of our departed heroes. The principal service for the day was the Solemn Requiem sung at 11. Punctually at the appointed hour the celebrant in alb and stole took his place at the High Altar with two attendant servers. After the celebrant had been vested with the chasuble the service opened with the Introit psalm, "Lord, thou hast been our refuge." Then came the ninefold Kyrie, the specially appointed collect, Epistle, and Gospel following in due order. The Nicene Creed was then murmured, the sermon coming in its place as laid down in the rubric.

The Vicar, the Rev. F. A. J. Bealey, preached, taking as his text a prayer of sublime beauty from an ancient Liturgy "Come, O Thou the Sanctifier, Almighty and Everlasting God, and bless this Sacrifice prepared for Thy Holy Name." In a discourse delivered with all the impressiveness the occasion demanded the preacher reminded his hearers that our holy faith was founded on sacrifice and that the fulness of the Catholic life could only be reached through sacrifice. He referred to those who from that parish had made the supreme sacrifice and that we were engaged in an act of divine charity on their behalf. "In the sight of the unwise they seemed to die and their departure is taken for misery and their going from us to be utter destruction; but they are at peace."

After the sermon the service proceeded on its usual course, the solemn moment of consecration being duly notified by the triple notes of the consecration bell. Before the service closed, the hymn, "Brother, now thy toils are o'er" was sung kneeling with great devotion. As the choir left the Church Bishop How's fine hymn "For all Thy Saints" was sung to a majestic tune taken from the English Hymnal. Evening devotions were sung at 6.30 and consisted of special psalms and prayers recited from the Altar. The latter half of Evensong followed, the sermon being preached by the Rev. R. B. Armstrong. The alms throughout the day were given to the St. Anselm's War Memorial Fund.

The names of the fallen heroes who were commemorated from the Altar by name are: Harry Hodgkins, Noel Atkinson, Edward Holderness, Eric Stearns, Leslie Jacobs, Jack Gill, Charles Hill, Gilbert Wood, John Aldridge, Michael Hill, Evers Leggatt Musgrave Webb, Charles Humphries, James Stevens, John Maddocks, Fred King, Arthur Heritage, Harold Gordon, Donald Hutchison, John Staniforth, George Brown, Fred Atkinson, Joseph Sowerby, Albert Segars, and Horace Crane.

Mr. C. M. Moxon, deputy-organist, very efficiently presided at the organ. The ladies’ choir gave useful help which it has done throughout the war period.

Stearns, E G
Eric Gordon Stearns was the son of Thomas Robert Stearns and was born on 23th May 1895. He joined the London Regiment.

Died from a wound, received while in charge of a digging party, digging a new trench. He was shot in the side and was killed from peritonitis.

He was a cheerful and fearless man, and was praised by all his comrades.

He was out digging at the front line during the night, a lot of bullets were being shot by the enemy and unfortunately one bullet hit him in the side and was immediately carried out by a motor ambulance to Aberle casualty station where the following night he died.

ST. JOHN'S COLLEGE ATHLETIC SPORTS.

The tenth annual athletic sports in connection with St. John's College (Greenlanes) were held on Wednesday afternoon at the Tufnell Park Athletic Grounds, Boston Avenue, before a large attendance of spectators. Magnificent weather smiled on the day, everything passing off well, and all concerned are to be congratulated. The following were the officials: — President, Mr. J. S. Coombes, M.A. (the Principal); committee, the Masters; judges, Messrs. J. B. Lee, A. Boley, F. Frankford; starter, referee, Messrs. F. T. Smith and H. G. B. Brewer; handicapper, Mr. E. R. Phelp; timekeeper, Messrs. Taylor and Davis. During the intervals the International Military Band, conducted by Louis Seidel, discoursed sweet music. The programme consisted of 23 events, some of which, perhaps, caused more excitement than the father's 300 yards scratch race, in which there can, and Mr. W. Bates cannot, compete now; nor is it easy to run that Old Boys 440 yards scratch, won by C. S. Wright, the winner accomplishing a fine performance, covering it in 55 secs.
